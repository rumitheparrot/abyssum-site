for the hard parts of being human.

Solitude, struggle, grief, and despair aren't new. Here are more ways people before you have sat with the dark and made something useful out of it.

On Solitude

Carl Jung and the Company of Yourself

"The privilege of a lifetime is to become who you truly are." — Carl Jung

Jung spent much of his career arguing that solitude isn't the absence of people — it's the presence of yourself, finally given room to speak. He believed most of what we call loneliness isn't about being physically alone at all. It's the exhaustion of being surrounded by people you can't actually be honest with. You can be lonelier in a crowded room than in an empty one, if the crowded room requires you to keep performing a version of yourself that isn't quite true.

He called the work of getting honest with yourself individuation — the slow, often uncomfortable process of integrating the parts of you that you've hidden, denied, or outgrown without noticing. Not fixing yourself. Meeting yourself. He thought this meeting almost always requires some real solitude to happen — not isolation as punishment, but solitude as a working condition, the way a photograph needs a darkroom.

What made his version of this bearable, rather than bleak, was his insistence that the parts of yourself you're most afraid to look at — what he called the shadow — usually aren't monstrous once you actually look. They're just unlit. Most of solitude's discomfort is the flinch before you turn the light on, not what's actually there once you do.

On the Fight

Nietzsche and the Use of Resistance

"That which does not kill us makes us stronger." — Friedrich Nietzsche

This line gets tattooed on people who've never read a word of Nietzsche past it, and it's usually misunderstood as toughness for its own sake — grit as a personality trait. That's not really what he meant. Nietzsche wasn't interested in suffering as proof of character. He was interested in what you do with it once it's already happened to you, since none of us get a choice about that part.

His deeper idea was amor fati — love of fate. Not resignation, and not forced positivity either. He meant something closer to: stop spending your energy wishing the difficult thing hadn't happened, and put that energy into becoming the kind of person who can hold it. He thought most people's suffering gets doubled — once by the event itself, and again by the endless argument with reality about whether it should have happened at all. The second suffering is optional. The first usually isn't.

He wasn't gentle about it, and he'd probably have no patience for a quote card. But underneath the intensity was a fairly practical idea: resistance is where strength actually gets built, the same way muscle only grows against something pushing back. The fight isn't a detour from the life you wanted. For most people, it's the only route there.

On Despair

Bukowski and the Ordinary Ruin of Things

"Find what you love and let it kill you." — Charles Bukowski

Bukowski didn't write about despair like it was a crisis to resolve. He wrote about it like weather — something you live inside of, not something you defeat and move past. That's part of why people who feel embarrassed by their own low points tend to find him strangely comforting. He never pretended the gutter had a silver lining. He just kept writing from inside it, which turned out to be its own kind of defiance.

His work is full of drinking, failure, bad jobs, and worse luck, and he almost never dresses it up. But underneath the mess is a genuine argument: that an undramatic, unglamorous kind of persistence — showing up to the typewriter, the racetrack, the next ordinary day — counts for more than people give it credit for. He wasn't offering hope, exactly. He was offering company, which for a lot of people going through something is closer to what's actually needed.

There's a version of strength that looks like winning, and a version that just looks like not stopping. Bukowski spent his whole career writing about the second kind, for people who'd never been offered the first.

On Shelter

Mary Oliver and the Small Yes

"Pay attention. Be astonished. Tell about it." — Mary Oliver

Oliver's attention is not a soft luxury. It's a way of gathering yourself into a feasible life when the world feels unruly. She doesn't promise that the storm will pass; she promises that the act of noticing is a way of staying present enough to live through it.

What she calls astonishment is really a practice of choosing what to keep close. Even when the dark is loud, a small yes — to the bird, the echo, the body part that still works — becomes an anchor. It's not about denying pain. It's about giving pain a landscape, so it doesn't swallow you whole.

Her writing is gentle, but the edge is in the insistence that attention is what turns a moment from chaos into something you can answer. That makes it a useful note for anyone who feels like their story is slipping through their fingers.

On Anger

Audre Lorde and the Use of Fire

"Anger is loaded with information and energy." — Audre Lorde

Lorde refused the idea that anger is a moral failing. For her, anger was a signal: something had been ignored, violated, or minimized. She demanded we treat it as data, not drama — a useful response, not a character flaw.

That makes her especially relevant when your story is not only painful, but also unjust. She shows how to hold the heat without letting it burn the thing you're trying to protect. Anger becomes a map of where your boundaries were crossed, and where your next honest line should be drawn.

Her tone is fierce and exact. If your own inner voice has been told to calm down, Lorde is the reminder that the work isn't to quiet yourself, it's to turn your anger into a clearer account of what you need.

On Quiet

Henry David Thoreau and the Inner Room

"Our life is frittered away by detail… simplify, simplify." — Henry David Thoreau

Thoreau's idea of solitude isn't about isolation from people. It's about creating the space inside you where your own values can be heard clearly. He treats quiet not as absence, but as the condition that lets the essential things come into focus.

When the story you're carrying is noisy or fragmented, his lesson is to clear enough room first. That doesn't mean avoiding the hard part. It means refusing to let the noise decide for you what matters, so you can see the pattern beneath it.

For people who feel crushed by too many choices or too much should, Thoreau offers a simple corrective: less clutter, more clarity. It's not the same as retreat; it's the work of making your next step intentional.

On Not Knowing

Rainer Maria Rilke and the Patience of Questions

"Be patient toward all that is unresolved in your heart." — Rainer Maria Rilke

Rilke doesn't rush you toward answers. His work is a permission slip for uncertainty — a way of saying that not knowing can be an honest place to start, not a failure. That can feel freeing if your story has been pressuring you to have it all figured out.

His version of growth is not a neat solution; it's the discipline of staying with the question long enough for it to change shape. In other words: you don't need a perfect conclusion to be doing something useful with what you're feeling.

That's the kind of framing that makes a submission less about proving you've thought enough, and more about making space for what the story wants you to see. Sometimes the most honest writing is simply the one that shows you are still listening.

On Habit

James Baldwin and the Long Work of Staying

"Not everything that is faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced." — James Baldwin

Baldwin knew that endurance is not heroic in a cinematic way. It's mundane, painful, and often unnoticed. The real work is not the single grand gesture, but the thousand small decisions to keep being yourself in a world that keeps asking you to be someone else.

That is useful for anyone whose story feels cyclical. Baldwin points to the fact that repetition is not always failure. Sometimes it's the only way to keep the light on long enough for the shape of the problem to become clear.

His voice is both patient and urgent. It's a reminder that if you keep returning to the same issue, the task isn't to judge yourself for looping — it's to name the pattern honestly and decide whether the next move is the same one or a different one.

On Care

bell hooks and the Radical Work of Tenderness

"Knowing how to be solitary is central to the art of loving." — bell hooks

hooks writes about care as a practice, not a feeling. She argues that the most radical thing you can do in hard moments is to treat yourself with the same attention and honesty you would give to someone whose story you were invited to hold.

That means recognizing when you've been asked to carry more than you should, and choosing to set boundaries anyway. It also means giving yourself permission to be gentle without letting gentleness become avoidance.

For a person who is tired of being told to be strong, hooks offers a different frame: that strength and tenderness are not opposites, and that the safest place to start is where you can still be honest with yourself about what you need.

On Memory

Joan Didion and the Work of Remembering

"We tell ourselves stories in order to live." — Joan Didion

Didion understood that memory is rarely a simple archive. It is selective, emotional, and often shaped by the very losses it tries to preserve. Her work asks us to admit that remembering is not neutral. It's a form of stewardship over what still hurts and what still matters.

That can be useful when a story feels too raw to speak plainly. Instead of forcing order onto it, she invites a steadier approach: notice the pattern, let the details remain uneasy, and give the truth enough space to be true.

The point is not to become detached from pain. It is to stop pretending the mind can be made clean by force. Some truths need to be held carefully, not solved.

On Return

Rebecca Solnit and the Practice of Coming Back

"The world is not over, and neither are you." — Rebecca Solnit

Solnit writes with the calm insistence that a life can be ruined and still remain worth returning to. Her work is especially helpful when despair has convinced you that the only sensible response is to disappear into it.

Her point is not that things will be easy. It is that there is a difference between a moment being dark and a life being finished. If you are still here, still breathing, still asking what is true, then the work of return has already begun.

That kind of perspective is quiet but powerful. It refuses a dramatic collapse and offers something more durable: the decision to keep going, one honest step at a time.

On Becoming

James Hollis and the Long Midlife of the Soul

"The task is not to find the answer, but to learn how to live with the question." — James Hollis

Hollis writes about the seasons of life when the role you played no longer fits, and the old certainties begin to loosen. That can feel like a breakdown. It is often, more accurately, the start of a deeper form of self-recognition.

His work is useful because it gives language to the feeling that growth is not always triumphant. Sometimes it is a slow stripping away of the stories you were taught to live by, until what remains is more honest and less curated.

When a life feels uncertain, he offers a steady reminder: the point is not to arrive at a perfect answer. It is to become someone who can live in truth without needing to hide from it.

On Second Chances

A Buddhist story about getting up again

"A wise man falls seven times and rises eight." — Japanese proverb

In many Buddhist stories, the point is not that the fall never happened but that the falling is not the end. The real practice is in the getting up, again and again, with the memory of what hurt and the intention to move differently.

That is a useful frame when your life feels like a series of hard stops. It means second chances are not something you wait for from the outside. They are the choices you make after the first attempt has failed, after the disappointment has landed.

These stories do not promise a clean restart. They simply offer a quieter permission: your next step can be kinder, wiser and more honest than the last one, even if the situation has not changed.

On Rebirth

What it means to choose again

"Every morning we are born again. What we do today matters most." — Buddha

The Buddhist idea of rebirth is often misunderstood as only about another life after this one. In practical terms, it can be read as the willingness to begin again in the same life, with the same body and the same story, but a little more awake.

That makes the work of recovery less mystical and more doable. It does not require that everything be different. It requires that you notice one pattern clearly enough to stop letting it run you, and then try another way.

If your current path has felt stuck, this perspective offers a gentle but powerful shift: the most meaningful rebirth may be the one that happens inside your own daily choices.
