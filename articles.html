Nature Is Medicine

On the Body's Own Logic

Ayurveda and the Idea That You Are Not Generic

"When diet is wrong, medicine is of no use. When diet is correct, medicine is of no need." — Ayurvedic proverb

Ayurveda is one of the oldest continuously practiced medical systems in the world, developed in India more than three thousand years ago, and its core premise is almost radical by modern standards: there is no universal "healthy." It starts from the idea that every person is built from a unique balance of three fundamental energies — vata, pitta, and kapha — and that real wellbeing means understanding your own particular balance, not following a one-size-fits-all rulebook.

This is why an Ayurvedic approach to something like insomnia, anxiety, or digestive trouble rarely looks like a single prescription. It looks like a question: what is out of balance for you specifically, and what combination of food, routine, rest, and herbs restores it. Modern wellness culture has borrowed a lot of Ayurveda's vocabulary — turmeric, ashwagandha, oil pulling — often without the underlying philosophy that gave those things meaning in the first place.

What's genuinely useful in it, stripped of trend, is the core reframe: symptoms aren't malfunctions to suppress, they're information about an imbalance. That's a fundamentally different relationship with your own body than most people are taught to have — less like fighting an enemy, more like listening to a conversation that's already underway.

On Unseen Pathways

Acupuncture and the Map Nobody Can See

"The needle is not the medicine. The needle is the question asked of the body." — Traditional Chinese Medicine teaching

Acupuncture is built on a map that doesn't appear in any anatomy textbook — a network of energetic pathways, or meridians, through which practitioners believe qi (vital energy) flows. Illness, in this framework, isn't primarily about broken parts. It's about blocked or imbalanced flow, and the needles are a way of asking the body to reroute or release what's stuck.

Western medicine has spent decades trying to translate this into language it can measure — nerve stimulation, endorphin release, blood flow changes — and has found real, reproducible effects, especially for pain and chronic tension, even without fully agreeing on the meridian model itself. That gap between "we don't fully understand the mechanism" and "it demonstrably helps a lot of people" is exactly where a lot of ancient medicine sits, uncomfortably, for a modern audience trained to want an explanation before they'll accept a result.

What acupuncture shares with a lot of the oldest healing traditions is a basic humility: that the body is a system, not a collection of separate parts, and that treating one point can move something much further away. Whether or not you believe in qi specifically, that systems-thinking — nothing in you exists in isolation — holds up.

On Will and Ceremony

Aleister Crowley and the Discipline Nobody Expects

"Every man and every woman is a star." — Aleister Crowley

Crowley's reputation precedes him — decades of tabloid scandal, deliberate provocation, and a public persona he cultivated himself have mostly buried the parts of his actual philosophy that were serious. Underneath the theatrics was a genuinely rigorous idea: that each person carries a specific, individual purpose — what he called their True Will — and that most human suffering comes from living out of alignment with it, whether from social pressure, fear, or simple inattention.

His famous, endlessly misquoted line, "Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the law," is routinely read as an endorsement of doing whatever you feel like. That's close to the opposite of what he meant. Discovering your actual will, in his framework, required intense discipline — ritual, meditation, self-examination — precisely because most of what people think they want is noise: borrowed desires, reactive impulses, other people's expectations wearing a disguise.

Whatever you make of the ceremonial magic and the deliberate scandal, the underlying question he spent his life circling is a fair one: how much of what you want is actually yours, and how much of it did you inherit from people you never agreed to imitate?

On Hidden Teachings

Manly P. Hall and the Habit of Actually Looking

"Everyone is a philosopher, though most men keep their reasoning within very close bounds." — Manly P. Hall

Hall spent his life doing something unfashionable even in his own time: reading everything, from Freemasonry to alchemy to Pythagorean number theory to Rosicrucianism, and refusing to dismiss any of it before understanding what it was actually claiming. His enormous work The Secret Teachings of All Ages, published when he was in his twenties, wasn't an argument for any single tradition. It was an argument for taking symbolic and esoteric thought seriously enough to study it properly, rather than writing it off as superstition without ever opening the book.

What made his approach distinct wasn't mysticism for its own sake — it was patience. He treated ancient symbol systems the way a careful translator treats a difficult text: assuming there was real meaning encoded in there, even if it took years to decode, rather than assuming confusion meant nonsense.

The habit worth borrowing from him isn't necessarily his conclusions. It's the discipline underneath them: sit with what you don't understand a while longer before deciding it's meaningless. Most people give up on a hard question long before Hall would have even considered himself to have started.

On Solitude

Carl Jung and the Company of Yourself

"The privilege of a lifetime is to become who you truly are." — Carl Jung

Jung spent much of his career arguing that solitude isn't the absence of people — it's the presence of yourself, finally given room to speak. He believed most of what we call loneliness isn't about being physically alone at all. It's the exhaustion of being surrounded by people you can't actually be honest with. You can be lonelier in a crowded room than in an empty one, if the crowded room requires you to keep performing a version of yourself that isn't quite true.

He called the work of getting honest with yourself individuation — the slow, often uncomfortable process of integrating the parts of you that you've hidden, denied, or outgrown without noticing. Not fixing yourself. Meeting yourself. He thought this meeting almost always requires some real solitude to happen — not isolation as punishment, but solitude as a working condition, the way a photograph needs a darkroom.

What made his version of this bearable, rather than bleak, was his insistence that the parts of yourself you're most afraid to look at — what he called the shadow — usually aren't monstrous once you actually look. They're just unlit. Most of solitude's discomfort is the flinch before you turn the light on, not what's actually there once you do.

On the Fight

Nietzsche and the Use of Resistance

"That which does not kill us makes us stronger." — Friedrich Nietzsche

This line gets tattooed on people who've never read a word of Nietzsche past it, and it's usually misunderstood as toughness for its own sake — grit as a personality trait. That's not really what he meant. Nietzsche wasn't interested in suffering as proof of character. He was interested in what you do with it once it's already happened to you, since none of us get a choice about that part.

His deeper idea was amor fati — love of fate. Not resignation, and not forced positivity either. He meant something closer to: stop spending your energy wishing the difficult thing hadn't happened, and put that energy into becoming the kind of person who can hold it. He thought most people's suffering gets doubled — once by the event itself, and again by the endless argument with reality about whether it should have happened at all. The second suffering is optional. The first usually isn't.

He wasn't gentle about it, and he'd probably have no patience for a quote card. But underneath the intensity was a fairly practical idea: resistance is where strength actually gets built, the same way muscle only grows against something pushing back. The fight isn't a detour from the life you wanted. For most people, it's the only route there.

On Despair

Bukowski and the Ordinary Ruin of Things

"Find what you love and let it kill you." — Charles Bukowski

Bukowski didn't write about despair like it was a crisis to resolve. He wrote about it like weather — something you live inside of, not something you defeat and move past. That's part of why people who feel embarrassed by their own low points tend to find him strangely comforting. He never pretended the gutter had a silver lining. He just kept writing from inside it, which turned out to be its own kind of defiance.

His work is full of drinking, failure, bad jobs, and worse luck, and he almost never dresses it up. But underneath the mess is a genuine argument: that an undramatic, unglamorous kind of persistence — showing up to the typewriter, the racetrack, the next ordinary day — counts for more than people give it credit for. He wasn't offering hope, exactly. He was offering company, which for a lot of people going through something is closer to what's actually needed.

There's a version of strength that looks like winning, and a version that just looks like not stopping. Bukowski spent his whole career writing about the second kind, for people who'd never been offered the first.
